Every summer, our cities seem to get a little hotter. In this talk, we will look at why that happens, and at the simple ideas that can help a city stay cool.

Let us start with a question. Why is the middle of a city often warmer than the countryside around it? Scientists call this the urban heat island effect. During the day, roads, roofs and walls soak up sunlight. Dark surfaces, like asphalt, absorb most of the energy that falls on them. At night, they slowly release that heat back into the air. In a field or a forest, the ground stays cooler, because plants shade the soil and release water into the air. So after sunset, the countryside cools down quickly, while the city stays warm for hours.

There is more. Cars, air conditioners and factories all release heat. Tall buildings block the wind, so warm air gets trapped in narrow streets. And when there are few trees, there is little shade for people walking or waiting at a bus stop.

Why does this matter? Heat is not only uncomfortable. On very hot days, more people need medical care, especially older people, small children and those who work outside. Hot nights make it hard to sleep, and tired people are less productive. Cities also use more electricity for cooling, which costs money and can put a strain on the power grid.

The good news is that we already know many ways to cool a city down. The first and simplest idea is to plant trees. A large tree can shade a whole section of a street. Its leaves release water vapour, which cools the air around it, a little like sweat cools our skin. Streets lined with trees can be several degrees cooler than bare streets nearby. Parks work the same way on a bigger scale. Even small pocket parks, built on empty corners, can make a difference to the people who live next to them.

The second idea is to change the colour of our surfaces. A white or light coloured roof reflects much of the sunlight back into the sky, instead of turning it into heat. In some cities, people paint their roofs with special reflective paint. The rooms below stay cooler, and the family uses less power for fans and air conditioning. The same idea can be used for pavements, playgrounds and parking areas.

The third idea is green roofs and green walls. A green roof is covered with soil and plants. It keeps the building below cooler, holds rainwater after a storm, and gives birds and insects a place to live. Green walls, covered with climbing plants, do the same for the sides of buildings.

The fourth idea is water. Fountains, ponds, canals and lakes all help to cool the air. Many old cities were built around water tanks and step wells for exactly this reason. Today, some cities are bringing back buried streams and turning them into green paths where people can walk and cycle.

The fifth idea is smarter building design. Traditional houses in hot regions often had thick walls, small windows, shaded courtyards and high ceilings. These features kept the inside cool without any machines. Modern architects are learning from these designs again. Windows that face away from the afternoon sun, wide balconies that cast shade, and gaps that let the wind flow through a building can all reduce the need for air conditioning.

Finally, cities can plan for heat the way they plan for floods. Some cities open cooling centres on the hottest days, such as libraries and community halls where anyone can rest in the shade and drink clean water. Others send text messages to warn people before a heat wave arrives, and change working hours for people who work outdoors.

People can help at home too. Closing curtains on the sunny side of the house during the afternoon keeps rooms cooler. A few pots of plants on a balcony give shade and cool the air near a window. Opening windows on two sides of a room at night lets the cool breeze flow through. And checking on an elderly neighbour during a heat wave can save a life.

None of these ideas is very expensive on its own. A tree, a can of white paint or a shaded bus stop may look small. But when thousands of people and many neighbourhoods do these things together, the whole city becomes cooler, healthier and more pleasant to live in.

So the next time you walk down a hot street, look around. Notice where the shade is, where the dark surfaces are, and where a tree or a light coloured roof could help. A cooler city starts with small choices, made one street at a time. Thank you for listening.
